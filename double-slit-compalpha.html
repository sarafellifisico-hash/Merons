The Double Slit and the Geometric Mechanism of Cyclic Interaction

Explanatory narrative for the interactive presentation

The problem behind the pattern

For more than a century, the double-slit experiment has been presented as a demonstration of so-called wave-particle duality. Light would produce interference because it behaves as a wave during propagation, yet it would be detected as a particle when it reaches the screen. In some interpretations, a single photon would pass through both slits or interfere with itself.

This description correctly predicts where the bright and dark bands will appear. It does not, however, display a physical mechanism explaining how this happens. What exists between the source, the slits, and the screen? What actually oscillates? How is an oscillation transmitted from one point to another? And how can something be both a localized particle and a wave distributed through space?

A geometric alternative

The Meronic Model proposes a different answer. It would not be necessary to assign a dual nature to light or imagine an entity that mysteriously alternates between wave and particle. Interference could be understood as the result of the oscillations of structures that are geometrically real within the model: the Compalphas.

Each Compalpha, or Ca, is a toroidal structure composed of an Alpha Point, an Alpha Axis, an Alpha Plane, and its Interaction Circles. These circles connect each Compalpha to neighboring Compalphas, forming a continuous mesh capable of transmitting Cyclic Interaction.

From S0 to the two slits

In this presentation, the Ca located at S0 acts as the pacemaker. When its Alpha Axis begins to oscillate, that oscillation is not carried by a tiny particle that leaves the source and travels through empty space. It is transmitted from Compalpha to Compalpha through the geometric connections between them.

The intermediate Ca receives the oscillation from S0 and transmits it to the Compalphas positioned at slits S1 and S2. From these two slits, two connected rows extend in straight lines toward the screen.

The animation uses only a few Compalphas so that the mechanism remains visible. In a real situation, according to the Meronic Model, an immensely greater number of these structures would fill the entire path. The displayed rows therefore do not represent trajectories of isolated particles, but a small sample of a much larger geometric mesh.

Mirrored oscillation

The fundamental rule of transmission is oscillatory mirroring. When the Alpha Axis of one Compalpha tilts in one direction, the Alpha Axis of the following Compalpha tilts in the opposite direction. If the first oscillates to minus ten degrees, the next oscillates to plus ten degrees. The third repeats the oscillatory orientation of the first, the fourth repeats that of the second, and so on.

The succession of Compalphas therefore creates an alternation between even and odd positions. This alternation preserves the oscillatory relationship along each row.

What happens at the screen

The result observed on the screen depends on the number of Compalphas contained in each path. The geometric distance between each slit and a particular point on the screen determines how many Compalphas are required to complete that row. A longer path contains one additional Compalpha relative to the shorter path.

At point 1, one row reaches the screen with an even number and the other with an odd number. Consequently, the two Alpha Axes arrive at the same Alpha Point with opposite oscillatory orientations. While one AA tilts upward, the other tilts downward. This opposition reduces the resulting amplitude of Cyclic Interaction, producing a point of attenuation.

At point 2, located symmetrically in relation to the two slits, the paths have the same length. Both rows contain the same number and the same parity of Compalphas. Their Alpha Axes reach the coincidence point with the same oscillatory orientation. The two contributions become concordant and the resulting amplitude is amplified. In the visualization, the AA produced by amplification appears violet, combining the blue and red used to distinguish the contributions from S1 and S2.

At point 3, the relationship observed at point 1 is reversed. The row originating at the other slit now follows the longer path and receives the additional Compalpha. Once again, an even number meets an odd number. The Alpha Axes oscillate in opposite directions and produce another attenuation.

Points 1 and 3: different parities produce oscillatory opposition and attenuation.
Point 2: equal parities produce oscillatory concordance and amplification.

The presentation allows the number of Compalphas to be increased or decreased without changing this relationship. When the reference-row quantity changes, the other row is recalculated automatically. At the central point, both quantities remain equal. At the lateral points, the row corresponding to the longer path receives one additional Compalpha.

From three points to interference fringes

In a true sea of Compalphas, these relationships would not occur at only three points. They would repeat continuously across the entire screen. At every position, the distances to S1 and S2 would be slightly different, gradually changing the oscillatory relationship between the two rows.

Some points would display almost complete concordance. Others would display almost complete opposition. Between them would lie every intermediate degree of amplification and attenuation. This amplitude gradient would be projected onto the screen as the succession of bright and dark bands known as the interference pattern.

The bands, in this interpretation, would not be produced by abstract sinusoidal waves passing through one another, nor by particles mysteriously interfering with themselves. They would be the macroscopic manifestation of the oscillatory relationships among countless interconnected Compalphas.

What the two representations show

The simplified mode shows each oscillating Compalpha as a compact local toroid with twelve meridian lines, making concordance and opposition easy to observe. In realistic mode, every numbered position is represented by a complete Metalpha Meron mesh. Each Metalpha contains twelve Compalpha structures oriented at the twelve vertices of a cuboctahedron, reproducing the intermediate geometric mesh used in the Cyclical Interaction presentation. The S1 ensemble is blue, the S2 ensemble is red, and the highly translucent meshes overlap freely without pair-by-pair CI sizing while preserving the mirrored oscillatory sequence.

The purpose of the simulation

This simulation is not intended to reproduce the actual number of Compalphas presupposed by the model. Its purpose is to make the proposed mechanism visible: the oscillation begins at S0, is transmitted to the intermediate Ca, divides geometrically at S1 and S2, travels through alternating rows, and coincides again at the screen.

What we call interference would, in this interpretation, be the relationship between oscillations that reach the same point by different paths. When they arrive in concordance, the amplitude is amplified. When they arrive in opposition, the amplitude is attenuated or, at the limit, canceled.

In this way, the Meronic Model replaces wave-particle duality with a continuous geometric mechanism. Light would not need to be a particle at one moment and a wave at another. What propagates would be Cyclic Interaction: an oscillation transmitted through a continuous mesh of connected Compalphas.

The double slit would therefore cease to be a demonstration of an incomprehensible behavior of nature and become the observable manifestation of an invisible geometric organization.

This presentation does not, by itself, constitute experimental proof of the existence of Compalphas. It shows that, once the structure proposed by the Meronic Model is admitted, it becomes possible to formulate a visual, continuous, and causal mechanism for the formation of the interference pattern—without resorting to the idea of an entity that must simultaneously be a wave and a particle.
